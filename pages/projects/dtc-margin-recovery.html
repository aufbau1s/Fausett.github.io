Margin Recovery for a $20M DTC Footwear Brand

2026

A $20M direct-to-consumer footwear brand came to me after revenue growth stopped translating into profit. The business had real demand. The problem was that its operating model had quietly become expensive and opaque — and no one had gone line by line to figure out exactly why.

I led the engagement through my holding company, working alongside my COO and our operations manager in China. Within seven days of first contact, we had a working diagnostic read on the business. Over the following two to three weeks, we built a full margin recovery roadmap. The roadmap identified up to $856K/year in annualized profit improvement and a separate working-capital opportunity in the six figures.

The problem

The brand wasn't failing because of bad products or weak demand. It was failing because complexity had outrun discipline. As the business scaled, costs accumulated faster than anyone was tracking them. Vendor relationships expanded. SaaS tools piled up. The org chart grew. Shipping policy was never revisited. And the supply chain — the most important cost driver in a physical goods business — was largely delegated to a third-party Chinese vendor with limited visibility into the underlying economics.

This is a common pattern in fast-scaling DTC brands. The founder is focused on growth. The team is executing. And the margin leaks compound quietly in the background until the P&L stops making sense.

What we found

The first job was to get a clear picture of where money was actually going — not at the summary level, but at the level of individual vendors, contracts, platform plans, headcount, and shipping rules. Here is what each category yielded.

Category Annualized opportunity Notes
Shipping policy $126K – $323K The free-shipping threshold had never been stress-tested against contribution margin. The highest-sensitivity lever in the model — outcome depends on how customers respond to the change.
COGS and supply chain ~$192K (follow-on) Manufacturing and fulfillment were bundled through a single third-party vendor with no visibility into bill of materials, freight, duties, or pick-and-pack economics. Roadmap created a path to direct sourcing and 3PL comparison.
Org structure ~$113K CS restructure ($75.6K/yr) and creative org cleanup ($37.2K/yr). Goal was to preserve throughput and customer experience while removing fixed costs not tied to measurable output.
Vendor and SaaS stack ~$181K+ Email agency ($36K), Klaviyo right-sizing ($24K), SMS platform switch ($78K+), and miscellaneous SaaS ($42.6K). Tools and retainers had accumulated without renewal discipline or clear ownership.
Marketing spend Qualitative Spend had expanded into TikTok and AppLovin without a clean source of truth for incrementality. Reset the decision standard from attributed revenue to contribution margin.
Cash management Six figures (working capital) Vendor payment timing and payables discipline were leaving liquidity on the table. Counted separately from annualized savings — this is cash unlocked, not cost removed.

The scenarios

The roadmap was built to be actionable: every line item came with an owner, a risk flag, and an implementation lane. The total opportunity ranged across three scenarios depending on how the highest-upside items landed.

Scenario Monthly Annualized
Conservative — core stack, low shipping ~$34.6K ~$415K
Base — with COGS / 3PL and creative follow-ons ~$54.6K ~$655K
Upper — base plus shipping at high end ~$71.4K ~$856K

All figures are identified and recommended annualized savings. Working-capital improvement sits outside these totals. Implementation verification is ongoing.

What I took away

The most interesting thing about this engagement wasn't any individual finding. It was how consistent the underlying pattern was: a business that had grown quickly, made reasonable decisions at each step, and ended up in a position where no one had a clear picture of the whole system.

Margin at scale is a systems problem. The individual line items — a SaaS tool here, a vendor retainer there, a shipping threshold that was set two years ago — don't look alarming in isolation. But they compound. And once a business is large enough that the founder can't personally track every cost, someone has to own the discipline of going back through the whole thing periodically and asking whether it still makes sense.

That's the work. It's not glamorous, but it's where the money is.
