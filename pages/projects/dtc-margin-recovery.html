Margin Recovery for a $20M DTC Footwear Brand

2026

A $20M direct-to-consumer footwear brand came to me after revenue growth stopped translating into profit. The business had real demand. The problem was that its operating model had quietly become expensive and opaque — and no one had gone line by line to figure out exactly why.

I led the engagement through my holding company, working alongside my COO and our operations manager in China. Within seven days of first contact, we had a working diagnostic read on the business. Over the following two to three weeks, we built a full margin recovery roadmap. The roadmap identified up to $856K/year in annualized profit improvement and a separate working-capital opportunity in the six figures.

The problem

The brand wasn't failing because of bad products or weak demand. It was failing because complexity had outrun discipline. As the business scaled, costs accumulated faster than anyone was tracking them. Vendor relationships expanded. SaaS tools piled up. The org chart grew. Shipping policy was never revisited. And the supply chain — the most important cost driver in a physical goods business — was largely delegated to a third-party Chinese vendor with limited visibility into the underlying economics.

This is a common pattern in fast-scaling DTC brands. The founder is focused on growth. The team is executing. And the margin leaks compound quietly in the background until the P&L stops making sense.

The diagnostic

The first job was to get a clear picture of where money was actually going. That meant pulling apart every major cost category — not at the summary level, but at the level of individual vendors, contracts, platform plans, headcount, and shipping rules — and asking whether each one was still justified.

The categories we worked through:

  • COGS and supply chain

    The brand's manufacturing and fulfillment were bundled through a single third-party vendor. No visibility into bill of materials, freight, duties, or pick-and-pack economics. We mapped a path to get that visibility and evaluate whether a more direct inventory and 3PL strategy could improve margin.

  • Shipping policy

    The free-shipping threshold had never been stress-tested against contribution margin. Adjusting it was the single highest-sensitivity lever in the model — a range of $10.5K to $26.9K per month depending on how customers responded.

  • Org structure

    The customer service and creative organizations had grown without clear accountability for output. We identified restructuring opportunities that preserved throughput and customer experience while removing fixed costs that weren't tied to measurable business value.

  • Vendor and SaaS stack

    The vendor stack had accumulated tools, retainers, and recurring charges without renewal discipline or clear ownership. Email agency, lifecycle platforms, SMS tooling, and miscellaneous SaaS were all running above what the business actually needed.

  • Marketing spend

    Spend had expanded into newer channels — TikTok, AppLovin — without a clean source of truth for incrementality. The brand was evaluating performance through attributed revenue rather than contribution. We reset the decision standard.

  • Cash management

    Vendor payment timing and payables discipline were leaving working capital on the table. Better cash visibility and payment sequencing could unlock six figures of liquidity without changing a single operating cost.

The output

The roadmap was built to be actionable, not theoretical. Every opportunity came with a monthly and annualized value, an owner, a risk flag, and an implementation lane. The goal was to hand the founder a document they could execute against — not a deck full of recommendations with no path forward.

The savings broke down across three scenarios:

Scenario Monthly Annualized
Core stack (conservative shipping) ~$34.6K ~$415K
With COGS / 3PL and creative follow-ons ~$54.6K ~$655K
Upper scenario (shipping lands at high end) ~$71.4K ~$856K

The working-capital opportunity — multiple six figures from vendor payment timing and cash management — sits outside these totals.

All figures represent identified and recommended annualized savings. Implementation verification is ongoing.

What I took away

The most interesting thing about this engagement wasn't any individual finding. It was how consistent the underlying pattern was: a business that had grown quickly, made reasonable decisions at each step, and ended up in a position where no one had a clear picture of the whole system.

Margin at scale is a systems problem. The individual line items — a SaaS tool here, a vendor retainer there, a shipping threshold that was set two years ago — don't look alarming in isolation. But they compound. And once a business is large enough that the founder can't personally track every cost, someone has to own the discipline of going back through the whole thing periodically and asking whether it still makes sense.

That's the work. It's not glamorous, but it's where the money is.
